# Chapter: Safe oxygen management

**Recognizing Hypoxaemia:**
Hypoxaemia (low levels of oxygen in the blood) is a life-threatening condition and can occur due to birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration, respiratory distress syndrome, severe pneumonia or sepsis.

**Use pulse oximetry whenever possible.**

It is recommended to help identify hypoxaemia and for guiding administration of oxygen therapy for neonates.

![Image](62cd062f2f5b4856be8db19f41ca9a28)

## Target saturation when giving oxygen therapy to newborns:

- **Full-term newborns:**92-97%  
- **Premature and/or birthweight:** 90-95 %

**Signs of hypoxaemia:**
If pulse oximetry is unavailable, look for these signs:

- Central cyanosis
- Nasal flaring 
- Inability to drink/feed (due to respiratory distress)  
- Grunting
- Drowsy and/or lethargic
- Severe lower chest wall indrawing
- Respiratory rate >60/minute for neonates
- Head nodding

## When to give oxygen:

- **Full-term:** SpO2 <90%
- **Premature and/or low-birthweight:** SpO2 <90%

**Recommended oxygen delivery methods:**

- Nasal prongs for newborns.  
- Avoid face masks and head boxes.
- Note: Consider CPAP if available or refer the newborn to a facility with CPAP.

**Standard oxygen flow rates:**
0.5-1 L/min (max 1 L/min without humidification.)

## Monitoring:

- Use pulse oximetry, where available.
- **Full-term:** normal SpO2 >88% in the first hours after birth, followed by 94-100%. 
- **Preterm (<32 weeks)**: Maintain 88% and 95% (avoid > 95%, to prevent retinopathy). 
- Discontinue oxygen: When stable at   >90% for at least 15 minutes of breathing in normal air.
- Discharge only when: Stable at >90% for at least 24 hours and all danger signs have resolved.

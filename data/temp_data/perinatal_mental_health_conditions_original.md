# Chapter: Perinatal depression

![Image](1ff402726a4d4bf0a655f67b50bea589)

Perinatal depression is a common condition in both the antenatal and postnatal periods. It often co-exists with anxiety.

Women with depression often feel unmotivated to seek treatment as they feel hopeless and shameful about their condition. They may have some level of depression outside of pregnancy and see their condition as normal. During pregnancy and the postpartum period, the risk of depression increases.

## Prevalence

Estimates suggest that 1 in 5 women will experience depression in the perinatal period.

## Symptoms

- Low mood
- Periods of weepiness
- Changes in appetite
- Low energy - general fatigue (e.g. sleeping/eating more or less than usual)
- Lack of joy & engagement in life
- Feelings of helplessness and hopelessness
- Confusion
- Poor cognitive functioning
- Can be quick to anger
- Difficulties to bond and interact with infant
- Feelings of guilt about mothering ability
- May have thoughts of suicide
- Non-specific body aches and pains and other physical symptoms

## Treatment

- First line management is engaging in a therapeutic relationship with women. 
- If her sleep is poor, try to help solve this problem first.
- CBT – talking therapy – empathetic counselling.
- Psycho-education – advising on healthy positive lifestyle choices. 
- Trauma informed care. 
- Increased social networks of support.
- Repeat the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale to monitor symptoms.
- For treatment with medication refer the woman to specialist care.

# Chapter: Anxiety disorders

![Image](2ac54a3153894be3bf70eb7cc2b3e391)

Most people experience anxiety from time to time; it is a healthy response to stress and can keep us motivated and safe. However, if anxiety becomes severe or prolonged and disrupts daily life activities, it is referred to as an anxiety disorder.

Anxiety disorders are common in the perinatal period, They are a group of related conditions that include:

- Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) 
- Panic disorder (PD) 
- Compulsive Obsessive Disorder (OCD) 
- Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

The common characteristic is a persistent and excessive fear or worry in situations that most people would not be disturbed by.

## Prevalence

20-25% of women experience anxiety in the perinatal period with increased rates leading up to the birth and into the postnatal and early parenting period.

## Commonalities

Most women will experience anxiety disorders as  pervasive feelings of stress, jitteriness, hypervigilance, fear, worry, lack of ability to control thoughts.

Anxiety disorders also cause a lack of appetite, disturbed sleeping patterns, fatigue, weepiness, loss of control, shame, inability to concentrate, and short temperedness.

Depression often coexists with anxiety disorders as women feel helpless to change their thoughts and reactions.

## Symptoms

- **General Anxiety Disorder:** Persistent and excessive worry about any number of events that can overwhelm and debilitate the woman. Repetitive negative thoughts that are distracting and create stress and fear for the woman.
- **Panic Disorder:**Extreme fear in anticipating an event or someone’s presence – results in sudden and recurrent panic attacks. Panic attacks are experienced as a rapid respiratory and heart rate, feeling overwhelmed with terror, and an inability to control the negative irrational thoughts.
- **Obsessive Compulsive Disorder:**Characterized by repetitive, unwelcome thoughts (obsessions) and illogical, extreme urges to perform specific actions (compulsions). For example, a deep fear of germs leading to multiple excessive hand washing. Although people with OCD can acknowledge that their thoughts and behaviours are not rational, they cannot control them.
- **Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder:**PTSD symptoms occur when a woman has distressing and persistent reactions following a traumatic event (e.g. a violent harmful event involving physical, sexual, psychological abuse, natural disasters, accidents). A previous traumatic birth or any unresolved stressful event may increase the risk of PTSD in the perinatal period. Women may experience flashbacks, nightmares, avoid certain places or people, withdraw socially, feel “numbness” or disconnected to life.

## Treatment

First line management is engaging in a therapeutic relationship with women.

- If her sleep is poor, try to help this problem first.
- CBT – talking therapy – empathetic counselling.
- Psycho-education – assisting with advising on healthy positive lifestyle choices. 
- Trauma informed care for women suffering PTSD – helping them process the shock/ trauma or grief. 
- Increased social networks of support.
- Repeat Edinburg Postnatal Depression Scale to monitor symptoms.
- Increased child family health services. 
- For treatment with medication refer the woman to specialist care.

# Chapter: Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder is a serious mental health condition. It is characterized by extreme mood swings that can last for up to a week or more and is difficult to manage without medication.

It is usually diagnosed in late adolescence or early adulthood. However, pregnancy can trigger an initial episode or a relapse.

There are two main categories of bipolar disorder: Manic and Depressive.

Some people with bipolar disorder can swing from the manic state to the depressed state with periods of stability in between. Others may only swing to one category.

## Prevalence

Rates for bipolar are 1-2% of the general population. The perinatal period can cause a relapse of bipolar disorder in 37% of women who have already been diagnosed with the disorder.

If the women are unmedicated the prevalence increases to 66%, however if they remain on medication the rate is 23%.

## Symptoms

- **Manic**- during this phase women have highly elevated moods with an agitated state of mind, often behaving erratically, with rapid speech patterns and requiring little sleep or food. They can report to be feeling euphoric. The women can also be irritable, exhibit poor impulse control and be difficult to reason with. When a woman is in a severe manic phase, she may experience hallucinations or be delusional and hear voices.
- **Depressive**- this period is expressed by a very low mood, negative feelings of hopelessness and despair with extreme sadness, and an inability to engage in or enjoy daily activities. During this phase of bipolar it is common to have suicidal thoughts.

## Treatment

- Psychoeducation
- Cognitive behavioural skills
- Lifestyle adjustments, regular sleep, and routines
- Increased child and family health services 
- For treatment with medication refer the woman to specialist care

# Chapter: Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is a serious, chronic, and complex mental health disorder that causes significant distress and decreased quality of life for sufferers and their families.

People with schizophrenia have increased rates of substance abuse, experience stigma and social isolation and often live in less than optimum housing.

Pregnancy can increase the symptoms of schizophrenia and trigger a psychotic event. It is crucial that these women are under obstetric and psychiatric care to monitor any adverse effects from the prescribed antipsychotic medications.

## Prevalence

Approximately 1-2% of the population suffer from schizophrenia.

## Symptoms

- Disordered thoughts 
- Delusional thinking (false beliefs)
- Hallucinations (usually auditory)
- Lack of insight into behaviours
- Disorganized lifestyle
- Social withdrawal 
- Paranoid behaviours- persecution beliefs
- Social anxiety
- Blunt affect
- Lethargy
- Unkempt – inability to care for self
- Abnormal behaviour - mumbling or laughing to self

## Treatment

- Psycho-education- particularly around the need for adequate sleep and nutrition.
- Supporting families in providing home care.
- Supportive child and family health services. 
- For treatment with medication refer the woman to specialist care.

# Chapter: Perinatal or puerperal psychosis

Perinatal psychosis is a rare and serious condition requiring urgent referral and almost always immediate hospitalization.

The onset of puerperal psychosis is often unexpected and rapid, usually occurring within 48 hours to 2 weeks of giving birth but may occur up to 12 weeks after the birth.

Women experiencing a perinatal psychosis will become very distressed and be unable to care for their infants. Women with this condition deteriorate rapidly and will need expert medical treatment.

## Prevalence

1:1000 women

This is rare, however as the condition is so severe that it is a major concern for maternal and neonatal mortality.

## Symptoms

- Rapid onset in early postpartum.
- Dramatic, erratic behaviour.
- Extreme mood swings.
- Can be extremely distressed by illogical thoughts and fears, often relating to inability to care for the baby.
- Hallucinations.
- Unable to think rationally or reason with.
- Cannot settle or sleep.

## Treatment

- Psycho-education- particularly around the need for adequate sleep and nutrition.
- Reassurance that the condition can be treated, as it is a very frightening experience for women. 
- Supportive child and family health services. 
- Assess risk for infant and mother. 
- For treatment with medication refer the woman to specialist care.
